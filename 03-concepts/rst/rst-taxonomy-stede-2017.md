# Rhetorical Relations Taxonomy (Stede et al., 2017)

This note consolidates the taxonomy of Rhetorical Structure Theory (RST) relations based on Stede et al. (2017). It explains them in my own words (so it's not even remotely academic), provides examples, and indicates connections with other theories, primarily Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL). The examples are a mixture of those used by the authors and my own.

## Strong Nuclearity
Before taking on the relations themselves, we must establish the principle of **strong nuclearity**. Normally, if you start with the nucleus of the root node and trace the structure down, you will find that the connection from the level above is almost invariably to the nucleus of the span below it. Basically, if you explore a well-built RST tree and just look at the nuclei, you get the gist—the "spine"—of the text (cf. Stede et al., 2017, p. 4).

This is important because the decision of what is a nucleus and what is a satellite in a node often depends on the logic of the *whole tree*. We appoint the nucleus from the standpoint of the node above it: whatever is selected as the nucleus must be the main idea that we want to propagate upwards.

## 1. Primarily Pragmatic Relations (The "Spin")
These relations organize ideas into an argument rather than reflecting natural events "out there". While some language simply describes the unfolding of material reality, these relations represent the language an author actively uses to structure their rhetorical arguments. This is the author putting a "spin" on the facts to align the reader, using what in SFL terms would be *internal conjunctions* (see: Martin & Rose, 2007) to manage the discourse itself.

*   **Background:** The author provides extra context in the satellite so the recipient can better "grasp" the main claim in the nucleus. This maps perfectly onto the principle of strong nuclearity: the main claim is in the nucleus because it is the most important "chunk of information" for the node above, so it propagates upwards.
    *   *Example (Stede et al.):* `[Until 1984, Burkina Faso was called Obervolta.]S [According to an EMNID poll, many Europeans today believe that they are two different countries.]N`
*   **Evidence:** The author makes a subjective-ish claim (nucleus) and substantiates it with what is arguably an objective fact (satellite).
    *   *Theoretical connection:* This maps perfectly onto Toulmin's model: the nucleus is the *Claim*, and the satellite provides the *Grounds/Data* (See: Toulmin, 2003). 
    *   *My example:* `[The recession seems to only be getting worse.]N [According to the official reports released in early May, the federal budget deficit for fiscal year 2026 has already been exceeded by almost 150%.]S`
*   **Reason:** Similar to Evidence, but the support provided in the satellite is another subjective-ish statement, not a hard fact.
    *   *Theoretical connection:* In the terminology of Martin and White's Appraisal framework, the truthfulness of the statement in the satellite is "vested in the author's subjectivity" (an *Entertain* orientation).
    *   *My example:* `[The recession seems to only be getting worse.]N [Most people feel like they now never have enough money for anything beyond just the bare necessities.]S`
*   **Reason-N:** The exact same logic as Reason above, but this time the reason is the *nucleus* because it forms the crux of the entire text span bubbling up to a higher node. In isolation, we might have called the main claim the nucleus. However, for the logic of the whole text (and specifically the node right above), the reason must be treated as the main point to maintain strong nuclearity.
*   **Justify:** The writer substantiates their *right* to make the main claim (nucleus) by presenting a fundamental political, moral, or personal attitude (satellite).
    *   *My example:* `[I am accelerating my timeline to secure secondary citizenship.]N [I fundamentally refuse to tie my future to a regime that operates with such blatant disregard for human rights.]S`
*   **Evaluation (Evaluation-N and Evaluation-S):** The satellite subjectively evaluates the nucleus on typical axiological coordinates (good vs. bad, desirable vs. undesirable). It comes in two flavors (Evaluation-N and Evaluation-S) depending entirely on which segment represents the "crux" of the argument for the node above.
    *   *My example:* `[The company has fired thousands of people again,]N [which totally sucks balls.]S`
*   **Motivation:** The nucleus presents an action the reader needs to take, and the satellite provides the psychological push or encouragement to do it.
    *   *My example:* `[You must leave the country as soon as possible, ideally in 2026,]N [because it may be too late later.]S`
*   **Enablement:** The satellite provides the concrete logistical steps or means that make actually performing the nucleus physically easier or more probable.
    *   *My example:* `[You must leave the country as soon as possible, ideally in 2026.]N [You can do this by preparing for Express Entry to Canada or by travelling to Argentina to pursue rapid citizenship.]S`
*   **Concession:** Two contrasting statements where there is a violated or failed expectation. The satellite is what is natural/expected, but the nucleus pushes past it to state what really is (we can use the "although" test to probe for this relation).
    *   *My example:* `[He's such a stand-up guy overall,]N [although he smokes and drinks like there's no tomorrow.]S`
*   **Antithesis:** The writer explicitly dismisses or rejects the satellite to reinforce the nucleus, typically in a "Not X, but Y" structure.
    *   *My example:* `[I now try to do active recall sessions]N [instead of just keeping "notes" stashed in my PKM system.]S`

## 2. Primarily Semantic Relations (The "Out There")
These relations describe objective states of affairs and factual connections in the material world, rather than structuring the author's argument. In SFL terms, these rely on *external conjunctions* (see: Martin & Rose, 2007) linking actual sequences of activities.

*   **Circumstance:** The satellite provides temporal or locational "framing" for the nucleus. (In SFL terms, this is an *Enhancing* relationship).
    *   *My example:* `[I can finish this post later]N [when I have more time.]S`
*   **Condition:** The satellite gives a hypothetical or future dependency under which whatever is stated in the nucleus happens. These are your classic EFL textbook conditionals.
    *   *My example:* `[If it rains,]S [we stay home.]N`
*   **Otherwise:** A preventative dependency. The nucleus is required to happen to prevent the hypothetical scenario in the satellite from happening.
    *   *My example:* `[They needed to contain the nuclear emission after the explosion;]N [otherwise, the whole region would have been contaminated.]S`
*   **Unless:** An exception dependency. Whatever is stated in the nucleus will happen *provided* whatever is in the satellite doesn't.
    *   *My example:* `[We'll stay home]N [unless the weather improves drastically.]S`
*   **Elaboration:** Adding specific details (moving down the cline of delicacy) to the state of affairs in the nucleus as a whole. Often based on set:item, class:species, or general:example relationships.
    *   *My example:* `[We're getting ready for the exit.]N [At this point, we're mostly doing research into the possible options.]S`
*   **E-Elaboration:** Expanding with details not on the whole nucleus, but on a *single entity* within it (e.g., using a non-defining relative clause).
    *   *My example:* `[I completely nuked my old PKM system,]N [which had turned into a massive cognitive scam that drained all my bandwidth.]S`
*   **Means:** The satellite answers "how" or by what method the realization of whatever is stated in the nucleus is made more probable.
    *   *My example:* `[He will land a PhD position in a decent Western university]N [by applying to every open vacancy he can find.]S`
*   **Purpose:** The satellite contains a hypothetical goal or state of affairs that will happen as a result of executing whatever is stated in the nucleus. The satellite justifies the execution.
    *   *My example:* `[In order to accomplish your goals,]S [you have to ruthlessly cut unnecessary activities and commitments.]N`
*   **Cause & Result:** Used for factive events where A leads to B. The mapping depends entirely on the logic of the node above.
    *   If the result is the most important part (Nucleus), it's **Cause**: `[He fell asleep within minutes]N [because he was extremely exhausted.]S` (_My example_)
    *   If the cause is the most important part (Nucleus), it's **Result**: `[He was extremely exhausted,]N [so he fell asleep within minutes.]S` (_My example_)
*   **Solutionhood:** The satellite presents the problem, and the nucleus provides the solution. This is somewhat counterintuitive because usually what is named by the label is found in the satellite (e.g., Cause = satellite is the cause). But here, the Satellite is the Problem. Solutionhood is "the idea" of the relationship, not the role of the satellite.
    *   *My example:* `[The liquor store is already closed.]S [We will have to find a place that sells after-hours booze.]N`

## 3. Textual Relations
These belong neither to the semantic nor pragmatic groups. Their sole purpose is reader-oriented: to "prep" and "prime" the reader, or to recap segments, helping to navigate the flow of the text.

*   **Preparation:** The satellite comes before the nucleus to announce "here's what's coming." It is short, contains minimal info, and acts as a warm-up.
    *   *Example (Stede et al.):* `[One thing is evident:]S [With each day of air raids, the military operations of the U.S. lose more and more credibility.]N`
*   **Restatement:** The nucleus precedes the satellite. The satellite restates the nucleus using *different wording*, but keeps the exact same length and level of generalization/delicacy.
    *   *Example (Stede et al.):* `[The mayor gave all the information to the councillors,]N [kind of filling them in completely.]S`
*   **Summary:** The satellite attaches to an underlying multi-unit span encompassing more than one nucleus, appending a short, condensed wrap-up.
    *   *My example:* `[[They didn't manage to unite the supporters.]N [They completely alienated those who were somewhat neutral as well.]N]N [In other words, they screwed up big time.]S` *(Note: The relations inside the first span are a Multinuclear List).*

## 4. Multinuclear Relations
Defined purely by structure, connecting two or more nuclei of equal weight.

*   **Contrast:** Similar to Antithesis and Concession, but without the "violated expectation" or "active dismissal". Connects *exactly two* comparable nuclei highlighting a difference.
    *   *Example:* `[The sky is blue,]N [whereas/while the clouds are white.]N`
*   **Sequence:** A real sequence of actions (chronological or reverse) reflecting the unfolding of material reality.
    *   *Example:* `[He came home,]N [poured himself some whiskey in a glass,]N [and guzzled the whole thing in one gulp.]N`
*   **List and Conjunction:** Multiple clauses in a row that do not form a temporal sequence, contributing to the text function in the exact same parallel way. If explicit connectives are used it is *Conjunction*; if none are used it is a *List*. (Note: mechanical EFL writing often dresses up a basic List as a more sophisticated rhetorical relation by slapping something like "furthermore" or "moreover" onto parallel items).
*   **Joint:** The **"Duct Tape"** relation (or the "Last Resort"). Used when two spans must both be nuclei globally, lack a specific semantic/pragmatic link, but aren't non-sequiturs either. They contribute equally to the text, so they are just juxtaposed. In the words of Stede et al. (2017): "Joint is to be used when a multinuclear relation is needed (from the text-global perspective) but none of the specific relations are applicable" (p. 21).
